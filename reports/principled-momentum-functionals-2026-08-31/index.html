Can gradient and Hessian histories determine a momentum kernel?

Theory palette · 31 August 2026

The ambitious version of the question has no solution. A finite training history cannot reveal a universally optimal momentum kernel. It can, however, determine a unique kernel for a declared task. The scientific work is to choose a task whose solution predicts training outcomes that were not used to define it.

Our leading proposal

Choose the causal filter that best estimates a high-token gradient in precisely those perturbation directions that can rotate Muon's polar update, with extra weight on rotations into sharp positive curvature. This gives a strictly convex objective and therefore one unique kernel. Newton then enters as a second, fallible estimator measured in the same metric.

1. What uniqueness can mean

Let a length-L causal kernel w produce the matrix

Mt(w)=∑τ=0L−1wτGt−τ.

Muon applies matrix sign to Mt. Multiplying every tap by the same positive number leaves that update unchanged. More generally, different pre-polar matrices can have the same polar factor. The data therefore identify equivalence classes unless we fix a convention. We use the gauge ∑τwτ = 1 and an explicit complexity penalty.

A second obstruction is more fundamental. Two objectives can have identical gradients and Hessians at every visited parameter while differing away from the trajectory. They can prefer different kernels after the first counterfactual update. No functional of one recorded path can remove this ambiguity.

The honest promise

We seek a unique minimizer of a stated empirical functional. We do not call it “the true kernel.” It earns practical meaning only by predicting held-out kernel rankings, schedules, batch dependence, and continuation loss.

The 129-lag Gram matrix is not the limiting factor in the present data. Its median participation ratio rises from about 30 to 55 to 102 across training, with more than 110 directions needed for 95% of its energy late. Many tap directions are numerically excited. Much of that rank may be fresh noise, so the hard problem is generalization to a changed trajectory rather than algebraic recovery on the recorded one.

2. A unique, msign-aware denoising kernel

2.1 The measured reference

At selected training states, evaluate many tokens at the same parameters and average their gradients. Call the resulting high-token gradient matrix St. It is a measured reference with sampling error, not a population oracle. Subsampling the same token pool gives its error curve as a function of token budget.

To express what Muon can see, use the regularized polar map

Pε(M)=M(MTM+εI)−1/2,ε>0.

The matrix inside the inverse square root is positive definite for every M, so Pε and its derivative exist everywhere. Let Jt be that derivative at St. Then JtE is the first-order change in the polar update caused by an estimation error E. Errors annihilated by Jt are locally invisible to Muon.

2.2 The functional

Let Ht,+ denote the positive-curvature part of the measured Hessian, acting on the vectorized parameter matrix. Define the positive-definite weight Ct = I + γHt,+. For a matrix of lagged gradients Xt, minimize

F(w)=∑tT∥Jt(Xtw−St)2Ct+ξ∥w∥2,subject to1Tw=1.

The first term asks the filter to estimate the high-token polar frame. The Hessian weight makes an error more expensive when its induced frame rotation points into sharp positive curvature. The second term fixes a unique representative and limits variance.

Existence and uniqueness

For fixed recordings, this is a quadratic function of w. Every Jt*CtJt is positive semidefinite, and ξ‖w‖² is strictly convex when ξ > 0. The affine gauge set is closed and nonempty. The functional therefore has exactly one minimizer, obtained by solving one constrained linear system.

This theorem concerns the regularized polar tangent. The hard matrix sign remains the deployed operation. We measure the approximation error directly by comparing hard-polar frames on held-out matrices and checkpoints. A large tangent-to-hard discrepancy kills the functional.

2.3 Kernel order and schedule

The uniqueness theorem first produces FIR taps. To ask whether those taps have a simple implementation, project them onto a nested sequence of fixed, canonical causal dictionaries: lag zero, a fixed exponential grid, and K-Maxwell banks with K = 4, 6, 8, …. Coefficients are unique within each fixed dictionary under the same ridge. Optimizing pole locations is nonlinear and is not covered by the theorem. Choose the smallest dictionary whose blocked held-out score is within one standard error of the best score. This is a declared complexity rule rather than an inferred law.

For scheduling, solve jointly for kernels at several training phases and add a quadratic penalty on changes between adjacent phases. The joint objective remains strictly convex. Interpolating the unique phase kernels gives a fully specified schedule. K8's raw coefficients are not the primary target because exponential mixtures have permutation and scaling ambiguities; its normalized impulse response is.

  1. Known-order retrodiction. On held-out states, the scores must recover K8 > bi-Maxwell > one EMA ≈ lag zero. This ordering is a test; nesting alone does not guarantee it out of sample.
  2. Batch endpoint. At 16× batch, the fitted nonzero-lag mass and the held-out advantage over lag zero should be statistically indistinguishable from zero.
  3. Schedule. Rolling fits should recover K8's annealed impulse-response trajectory without using K8 coefficients or outcomes during fitting.
  4. Visibility. K8's advantage should appear in hard-polar frame error and curvature along the update. A gain visible only before matrix sign is irrelevant.

3. A different target: the best action in hindsight

The denoising functional assumes the high-token current gradient is the right object to estimate. A second functional asks which single local action would have looked best given the next N recorded gradients and Hessians. Define

ut*=argminu[∑h=0N−1ωh(⟨gt+h,u⟩+12uTHt+hu)+ρ2∥u∥2].

Choose ρ above the magnitude of the most negative eigenvalue of the weighted Hessian sum, plus a fixed positive margin. The objective is then strongly convex, so ut* exists uniquely. Fit a causal kernel to these actions with the same strictly convex regression used above.

This target incorporates future curvature and persistent descent rather than declaring a Newton solve perfect. It also has a serious limitation: the future derivatives were recorded along the baseline trajectory, not the counterfactual trajectory the new kernel would generate. The functional is a local ranking device. Its solution must transfer across time blocks and survive actual continuations.

Horizon rule

One-step selection previously reversed after continuation. We therefore fit several declared horizons and accept a kernel only when its blocked ranking stabilizes. The horizon is not assumed to be a universal constant; it may vary with learning rate, batch, and training phase.

4. Make either functional robust to the realized path

An average fit can exploit one phase of training. Partition the history into blocks using the measured Hessian-frame drift curve, and let Fb(w) be the polar-tangent or hindsight loss on block b. The robust kernel solves

minwmaxbFb(w)+ξ∥w∥2.

A maximum of convex quadratics is convex; the positive ridge makes it strictly convex. A unique minimizer therefore exists. A smoother H∞-style variant takes the worst case over the convex hull of measured gradient residuals and Hessian-drift increments. Compact measured envelopes give existence by Weierstrass; the same ridge gives uniqueness.

The robust version makes a sharp tradeoff. Extra K8 poles survive only when they help multiple phases or protect against a measured disturbance extreme. If they merely interpolate fresh late-training noise—the lag Gram's participation ratio reaches 102—they should disappear.

5. Momentum and Newton as fallible partners

Use the same high-token reference and polar-tangent metric to measure two error curves:

Temporal estimatorError versus history length, kernel shape, and Hessian-frame staleness.
Curvature estimatorError versus tokens used to estimate curvature, Hessian–vector products, damping, and refresh age.

The errors are correlated because both estimators use the same gradients. Simple inverse-variance weighting is therefore unjustified. For each state, estimate their two-by-two error covariance and biases from token subsamples and held-out blocks. Generalized least squares gives the unique minimum-error linear combination when the covariance is positive definite; adding a declared ridge gives a unique solution when it is singular.

Newton remains design-time-only in the current commission. The combined estimate becomes a target, and we project it back into the causal-kernel class using the lead functional. The projection residual answers whether the proposed compensation is deployable without Hessian access. If runtime Newton is later allowed, that is a separate optimizer class.

  1. The weight assigned to curvature correction should rise with curvature-token and HVP budget, then fall as the estimate becomes stale.
  2. The optimal temporal memory should grow as gradient SNR falls, but cannot exceed the measured frame-coherence time.
  3. At 16× batch, the incremental value assigned to temporal filtering should reach zero on the measured 750-step task.
  4. If the projection of the combined estimator into causal kernels has large held-out error, Newton and momentum cannot be integrated under the deployment constraint; no amount of coefficient tuning repairs that.

6. In what order should we average gradients and curvature?

Jeffrey's question

“Suppose we actually were given a stream of finite-token Hessian estimates and a stream of gradients. What is the optimal ordering of gradient accumulation, Hessian accumulation, preconditioning, and msign? Is it kosher to combine gradients preconditioned by different Hessians at different points in time?”

There is no ordering that is optimal without assumptions about metric drift and estimator error. There is, however, a clean way to distinguish the choices.

Let Mt be a positive-definite curvature metric: for example, a damped or clipped Hessian, or a GGN estimate. Writing Ag and AM for causal accumulators gives four apparent orderings:

OrderingPre-polar directionInterpretation
1AM(M)−1 Ag(g)Average covectors and metrics, then raise the averaged covector with the current averaged metric.
2Ag(Mt−1gt)Raise every noisy covector with its same-step noisy metric, then average vectors.
3M̄t−1ḡtThe same algebra as ordering 1 when the bars denote those same accumulators; it differs only if the windows or update schedules differ.
4∑τkτM̄t−τ−1gt−τRaise each covector with a contemporaneously smoothed metric, then carry the resulting vectors forward and average them.

6.1 The type question

In fixed neural-network coordinates, parameter space is affine. Translation gives a canonical identification of tangent spaces at different parameter values, and the dual coordinate basis similarly identifies cotangent spaces. Ordinary momentum already uses this trivial connection when it adds gradients from different steps. Thus all four formulas type-check as arrays in fixed coordinates.

They are not invariant to a change of parameterization. On a genuinely curved statistical manifold, a past covector or vector must be transported before it is combined with a current one. Ordering 1 implicitly transports past covectors by identity and raises them using the current metric. Ordering 4 raises each covector using its historical metric and then transports the resulting vector by identity. These are different connection choices, not merely different numerical schedules.

6.2 The fixed-metric lemma

If Mt = M is constant and all accumulators use the same linear weights, preconditioning commutes with accumulation:

M−1∑τLkτgt−τ=∑τLkτM−1gt−τ.

All meaningful differences therefore come from metric drift, unequal accumulator windows, finite-token estimation error, or nonlinear operations inserted between the sums. The answer necessarily depends on the gradient kernel's memory: a ten-step kernel sees little metric drift; a thousand-step kernel may span a different geometry.

6.3 Why averaging the metric before inversion is the safe default

Matrix inversion is operator-convex on the positive-definite cone. For an unbiased positive-definite estimate M̂,

E[M̂−1]≥(E[M̂])−1.

Inverting each small-token estimate therefore amplifies metric noise on average. This makes ordering 2 a poor default. The statement has limits: it requires a positive-definite metric, and it does not account for correlation between M̂ and the gradient estimated from the same tokens. An indefinite Hessian must first be converted into a declared damped, clipped, or trust-region metric. Ordering 2 is not forbidden by a universal theorem; it must overcome a measurable Jensen gap and a same-sample correlation term.

6.4 The real fork: current metric or historical metric?

After smoothing curvature before inversion, the substantive choice is ordering 1 versus ordering 4. Their difference is

∑τLkτ(M̄t−τ−1−M̄t−1)gt−τ.

If the observed metric movement is mostly finite-token noise, one current smoothed metric should be applied retroactively to the whole gradient buffer: ordering 1. If it is mostly genuine rotation of useful features, historical preconditioning may preserve information that a current metric would reinterpret incorrectly: ordering 4, ideally with an explicit transport rather than identity transport.

The measured top-eight subspace overlap over 250 steps rises from 0.24 early to 0.86 late. This predicts a real ordering dependence early and near-indifference late, but only after subtracting the curvature-estimation noise floor. Optimizers that rotate momentum buffers when a preconditioner basis is refreshed implement an approximate discrete transport. A basis refresh without buffer rotation does not.

6.5 Where matrix sign belongs

For the estimator architecture studied on this page, matrix sign comes last: first estimate one pre-polar covector or vector, then apply the norm-selection map. Applying matrix sign to every step before accumulation discards magnitude and conditioning information before the temporal estimator can use it.

Accumulating already-polarized updates is still a type-correct optimizer in fixed coordinates. It is momentum in primal update space rather than gradient momentum, and it requires its own transport rule and objective. It should be tested as a separate family rather than treated as another algebraic ordering of the same estimator.

Measurement that decides the fork

At each checkpoint, estimate curvature frames twice from independent token subsets. Their same-step disagreement is the estimator-noise floor. Compare it with cross-step frame disagreement at several lags. The excess cross-step disagreement estimates true drift. Combine that curve with the candidate gradient kernel's age distribution. Ordering 1 is favored when noise dominates over the ages carrying most kernel weight; ordering 4 or explicit transport is favored when true drift dominates.

7. Two useful diagnostics that are not yet theories

Frozen-disturbance replay LQ

In a tracked positive-curvature eigendirection, freeze the recorded scalar-gradient sequence and integrate a counterfactual filtered update through the recorded curvature sequence. For a finite FIR basis the state is affine in the taps, so accumulated positive-curvature cost is a positive-semidefinite quadratic. Ridge makes its minimizer unique and closed form.

This is cheap and may reveal an alternating tap or a curvature-dependent gain elbow. It is valid only as a local diagnostic near the kernel that generated the path. Richer nested bases necessarily fit its in-sample quadratic at least as well; that fact does not explain why K8 lowers validation loss. Held-out time blocks and real continuations carry the explanatory burden.

A regularized self-consistent kernel

Let T(w) be the unique response of one of the convex functionals when evaluated on a trajectory generated by w. With deterministic data order, a smooth polar map, and a compact convex normalized FIR class, the trajectory and response maps can be made continuous. Brouwer's theorem then gives at least one fixed point T(w) = w. It does not give uniqueness. Hard matrix sign, stochastic data, or a nonunique response invalidates the existence argument unless regularized first.

This construction addresses trajectory endogeneity in principle but is expensive: evaluating T requires new training trajectories. One off-policy update using an old trajectory is an approximation, not the fixed-point map.

9. The shortest experimental program

  1. Fit offline from existing recordings. Compute the polar-tangent quadratic, rolling unique kernels, robust variants, and frozen-replay diagnostic. No training run is needed.
  2. Run the retrodiction gate. Hold all K8 weights and outcomes out. Ask whether the fitted impulse responses recover K8 > bi-Maxwell > EMA = lag zero, K8's annealing direction, and the batch curve ending at zero at 16×.
  3. Resolve the ordering fork offline. Subtract same-step, independent-token curvature-frame disagreement from cross-step disagreement, then integrate the remaining drift over each candidate kernel's age distribution. This selects current-metric accumulation, historical-metric accumulation, or explicit transport without a training run.
  4. Collect the missing same-state references. Where existing microbatch pools are insufficient, evaluate more tokens at frozen checkpoints and compute subsampled high-token gradients and curvature estimates. This measures both error curves without a parametric noise model.
  5. Race only preregistered survivors. Compare the derived kernel, K8, bi-Maxwell, and lag zero in matched short continuations from shared checkpoints. Use at least one held-out training phase before a full Track 3 run.
  6. Compress. If rolling unique FIR solutions lie near a low-order exponential manifold, factor them into the smallest K-Maxwell bank that preserves hard-polar behavior. If they do not, the theory predicts that a zero- or one-knob exponential implementation does not exist on this stack.
What would end this program?

If none of the unique history functionals retrodicts K8's held-out ordering and schedule, full gradient and Hessian histories are insufficient under these declared utilities. The correct response would be to stop deriving kernels from local histories and return to direct continuation optimization—not to invent a more elaborate latent oracle.

Notation: λ remains reserved for curvature throughout the project; ξ denotes ridge strength and ρ denotes damping. All claimed uniqueness is conditional on the displayed gauge, kernel class, and positive regularization.
