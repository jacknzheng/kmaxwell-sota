How do momentum and Newton interact?

Research proposal · revised 1 September 2026

Scheduled K8—a momentum kernel built from eight exponential buffers—beats bi-Maxwell, which combines two exponential buffers, while a single exponential moving average provides no measurable benefit over no momentum. Bi-Maxwell's benefit shrinks with batch size and reaches a measured zero at \(16\times\) batch. The useful part of momentum therefore depends on temporal shape and on gradient noise; plain averaging does not explain it. Adding Newton introduces a second operator that acts on many of the same gradient directions. The question is where the two operators duplicate each other and where they contribute independent information.

How to read this proposal

This is an offline design-time analysis. Future gradients and Hessians define a reference action after a training trajectory has been recorded; the deployed optimizer never receives them. The first test is cheap: the offline score must reproduce the known ordering K8 \(>\) bi-Maxwell \(>\) single EMA \(\approx\) no momentum. If it does, offline fits can replace continuation sweeps when selecting kernels. If it does not, discard the framework.

1. Regularized steepest descent supplies the offline target

This section starts from one-step steepest descent, extends the same local model across recorded future states, and states what success would buy us.

Write the parameter update as \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-u\), where \(u\) is a descent matrix. Regularized steepest descent in a positive-definite metric \(R_t\) chooses

\[ u_t^{\mathrm{SD}} =\underset{u}{\operatorname{argmin}} \left[-\langle g_t,u\rangle+\frac12u^\top R_tu\right] =R_t^{-1}g_t. \]

Choosing \(R_t=H_t+\delta I\) uses the local Hessian as the metric. The damping coefficient \(\delta\) is large enough to make \(R_t\) positive definite. This gives the familiar damped-Newton direction \((H_t+\delta I)^{-1}g_t\).

The hindsight action asks which single descent matrix would score well under local models from several recorded future states. It does not simulate applying that matrix repeatedly. Let \(q_h\ge0\) be the declared weight of future offset \(h\), with \(\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_h=1\). Define

\[ u_t^*=\underset{u}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_h\left(-\langle g_{t+h},u\rangle+\frac12u^\top H_{t+h}u\right)+\frac{\delta}{2}\lVert u\rVert^2\right]. \]

This action rewards descent that persists across the window and penalizes directions with sustained positive curvature. When \(N=1\) and \(q_0=1\), it reduces to the one-step regularized steepest-descent action above.

Derive the closed form

Differentiating the objective with respect to \(u\) gives

\[ -\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_hg_{t+h} +\left(\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_hH_{t+h}+\delta I\right)u=0. \]

Define the future-window metric

\[ B_t=\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_hH_{t+h}+\delta I. \]

If \(B_t\) is positive definite, the unique stationary point is the unique minimizer:

\[ u_t^*=B_t^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}q_hg_{t+h}. \]

The quadratic penalty is the Lagrangian form of a trust-region constraint: for a chosen \(\delta\), the solution has some radius \(r=\lVert u_t^*\rVert\). It is not the same as imposing the numerical radius \(\delta\).

The horizon \(N\), future weights \(q_h\), and damping rule are part of the offline target. They are fixed before any held-out kernel score is inspected. If the known kernel ordering is stable across nearby declared horizons, the target becomes a cheap kernel-selection instrument; if the ordering fails, the target is discarded.

Assumption under test

Gradients and Hessians recorded along the baseline trajectory must preserve the ranking of nearby counterfactual kernels within a training phase. Fitting on earlier blocks and predicting later blocks tests this assumption. Short training continuations provide the final check.

The calculation is an offline design expense. A matrix-free solve that takes \(k_{\mathrm{solve}}\) Krylov iterations over \(N\) future Hessians costs \(k_{\mathrm{solve}}N\) Hessian–vector products per state. Direct optimization within a \(K\)-parameter kernel family instead costs \(O(KN)\) Hessian–vector products to form the small quadratic.

2. The controller target constrains temporal filtering

This section states what a momentum kernel must reproduce before its interaction with curvature inversion can be interpreted.

Let \(G_{t-\tau}\) be the gradient matrix observed \(\tau\) steps ago. In Muon's fixed Euclidean coordinates, the gradient matrix is the unique matrix whose Frobenius inner product with any step \(v\) equals the directional derivative in direction \(v\). This identifies gradient covectors and step matrices for the regression; Section 4 shows how the two underlying types transform. A length-\(L\) causal kernel assigns one weight \(w_\tau\) to each available lag:

\[ M_t(w)=\sum_{\tau=0}^{L-1}w_\tau G_{t-\tau},\qquad \sum_{\tau=0}^{L-1}w_\tau=1. \]

Muon uses the polar factor of \(M_t\): the semi-orthogonal matrix returned by matrix sign. To compare nearby matrices smoothly, define the regularized polar map

\[ P_\varepsilon(M)=M(M^\top M+\varepsilon I)^{-1/2},\qquad\varepsilon>0. \]

Let \(\mathcal J_t\) be the Jacobian of \(P_\varepsilon\) evaluated at \(u_t^*\). The product \(\mathcal J_tE\) is the first-order change in the polar update caused by a small matrix error \(E\). This Jacobian may have a null space: changing singular values without changing singular vectors can leave the polar factor unchanged to first order. Penalizing those invisible changes would optimize details that Muon discards.

For fitting states \(\mathcal T\), choose the kernel that minimizes the squared change in Muon's polar update:

\[ \Phi(w)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\left\lVert\mathcal J_t\bigl(M_t(w)-u_t^*\bigr)\right\rVert_2^2+\xi\lVert w\rVert_2^2,\qquad \xi>0. \]

Every data-fit term is quadratic and positive semidefinite in \(w\); the ridge term is strictly convex. The normalization constraint is affine, so exactly one kernel minimizes \(\Phi\).

Separate kernels can be fitted for early, middle, and late training. A quadratic penalty on changes between adjacent phase kernels produces a unique schedule. The resulting lag weights can then be approximated by the smallest practical exponential mixture whose held-out score is unchanged.

Required known-ordering test

Without using validation losses during fitting, the functional must rank scheduled K8 above bi-Maxwell, and bi-Maxwell above both single-EMA momentum and no momentum. It must also predict the direction of K8's annealing. Failure on either test ends this formulation.

3. Momentum and Newton must be composed

This section defines Newton's role in the composition and states exactly which source of sampling variance the offline comparison measures.

Momentum maps a sequence of gradient covectors to one momentum matrix \(M_t(w)\). Damped Newton then maps a covector to a step vector with

\[ C_t^{-1}=(H_t+\rho I)^{-1}, \]

where \(\rho\) makes \(C_t\) positive definite. Applying \(C_t^{-1}\) before or after temporal accumulation gives the same result only when the same linear curvature map is used at every lag. Section 4 defines the types and lists the distinct compositions when the metric changes.

The sampling comparison redraws only the current finite-token minibatch. The parameter state, recorded past gradients, and high-token derivatives that define \(u_t^*\) remain fixed. For redraw \(r\) and composition \(p\), let \(x_{p,r}\) be the matrix entering the final matrix-sign operation. Its error relative to the polar-normalized hindsight action is

\[ e_{p,r}=P_\varepsilon(x_{p,r})-P_\varepsilon(u_t^*). \]

Bias, variance, and total mean-squared error separate systematic mismatch from current-minibatch noise:

\[ \operatorname{bias}_p^2=\left\lVert\mathbb E_r[e_{p,r}]\right\rVert_2^2,\qquad \operatorname{variance}_p=\mathbb E_r\left\lVert e_{p,r}-\mathbb E_r[e_{p,r}]\right\rVert_2^2, \qquad \operatorname{MSE}_p=\mathbb E_r\lVert e_{p,r}\rVert_2^2. \]

The identity \(\operatorname{MSE}_p=\operatorname{bias}_p^2+\operatorname{variance}_p\) checks the measurement. The frozen history is one shared noisy realization, so it can bias every composition in the same direction; this experiment does not estimate variance over entire training trajectories. Repeating the comparison across matrices, checkpoints, and independently recorded histories is the available safeguard.

4. Types determine which compositions are legal

This section defines gradients as covectors, uses their transformation law to constrain the pipeline, then derives the curvature-averaging window.

A covector is a linear function that maps a possible parameter step to a scalar. The gradient \(g\) is the covector defined by the directional derivative: for a small step \(v\),

\[ Df(\theta)[v]=g^\top v. \]

This definition explains how gradients change under a reparameterization. If \(\theta'=S\theta\) for an invertible matrix \(S\), then a step transforms as \(v'=Sv\), while the gradient transforms in the opposite way:

\[ g'=S^{-\top}g,\qquad {g'}^\top v'=g^\top v. \]

Thus steps are vectors and gradients are covectors. Covectors may be added to covectors, and vectors may be added to vectors. A curvature inverse \(A^{-1}\) converts a gradient covector into a step vector. Adding a raw gradient to a preconditioned gradient requires an explicit identification such as the Frobenius choice in Section 2, and the result changes under reparameterization. Composition keeps the conversion point explicit.

Neural-network parameters use a fixed affine coordinate system, which canonically identifies gradients from different training steps by translation. Ordinary momentum uses this identification when it adds historical gradients. On a nonlinear reparameterization, that implicit transport rule changes, as the transformation law above shows.

Use one time index throughout. Let \(A_t\) be the raw positive-definite curvature metric at step \(t\), let \(\widetilde A_t\) be its time-smoothed value at that step, and let \(\bar A=\mathbb E[A_t]\) be the mean across independent metric estimates at one fixed parameter state. Let

\[ \widetilde g_t=\sum_{\tau=0}^{L-1}\alpha_\tau g_{t-\tau} \]

be the accumulated gradient covector. The temporal weights are \(\alpha_\tau=w_\tau^*\), where \(w^*\) minimizes the Section-2 objective \(\Phi\); the composition analysis does not tune them again. The distinct orderings are

ExpressionMeaning
\(\widetilde A_t^{-1}\widetilde g_t\)Smooth curvature and gradients separately, then use the current smoothed metric.
\(\sum_\tau\alpha_\tau A_{t-\tau}^{-1}g_{t-\tau}\)Use each raw same-step metric, then accumulate step vectors.
\(\sum_\tau\alpha_\tau\widetilde A_t^{-1}g_{t-\tau}\)Expand the first expression as a sum; this is the same composition.
\(\sum_\tau\alpha_\tau\widetilde A_{t-\tau}^{-1}g_{t-\tau}\)Use the smoothed metric available with each historical gradient, then accumulate step vectors.

With a fixed positive-definite metric, all four expressions coincide. When the metric changes, the first expression converts every historical gradient using the current metric; the fourth preserves the step vector implied when each gradient was observed. The second additionally inverts an unsmoothed metric at every step. For a random positive-definite snapshot metric \(A_t\), operator convexity of inversion gives

\[ \mathbb E[A_t^{-1}]\succeq\bar A^{-1}, \]

where \(X\succeq Y\) means that \(X-Y\) is positive semidefinite. Inverting each noisy metric before averaging can therefore amplify low-curvature directions. This argument concerns noise in the metric alone; correlation between a same-minibatch metric and gradient can change the total error. The pipeline MSE from Section 3 makes the final comparison.

A longer metric window reduces sampling noise but uses older curvature. For a trailing rectangular window of \(W\) steps, assume local linear drift of size \(d_A\) per step and independent same-step estimation variance \(\sigma_A^2\), measured in one matrix distance. The leading mean-squared error and its minimizer are

\[ E(W)=\frac{\sigma_A^2}{W}+\frac{d_A^2W^2}{4},\qquad W^*=\left(\frac{2\sigma_A^2}{d_A^2}\right)^{1/3}. \]

Estimate \(\sigma_A^2\) from independent token subsets at the same parameters. Estimate \(d_A\) from adjacent checkpoints after subtracting that same-step noise floor. At the measured token budget, independent same-state eigenspaces overlap by only \(0.46\)–\(0.58\); after subtracting this noise, true rotation is detectable early and not late. Token count therefore limits the curvature estimate throughout training, while staleness matters mainly early.

No row in the table is currently recommended. The measured noise–drift tradeoff determines the curvature window. After the fixed-metric test in Section 5, repeating the same factorial with \(\widetilde A_t\) and with the step-matched \(\widetilde A_{t-\tau}\) determines whether row 1 or row 4 preserves more controller value. The \(\alpha_\tau\) weights come from Section 2.

Within this architecture, apply Muon's matrix sign after temporal aggregation and curvature transformation. Accumulating already-polarized updates defines a separate optimizer.

5. Does Newton change which momentum kernel is best?

This section asks the interaction question directly, reports what the same-state analysis has established, and explains why the task-level test needs denser recordings.

If the best kernel is unchanged by damped inverse-Hessian preconditioning, temporal and curvature design separate into two smaller problems. If the best kernel changes, the interaction still constrains the design: it identifies which temporal degrees of freedom Newton makes redundant and which remain useful.

The cheapest test crosses two gradient summaries with two curvature operators at a fixed checkpoint. Let \(g_t\) be the current gradient, let \(m_t^{\mathrm{K8}}\) be the phase-matched K8 momentum matrix formed by the K8-weighted sum of recorded gradients, and let \(C_t=H_t+\rho I\) be the damped Hessian, with \(\rho\) set to the eighth measured Ritz curvature. The four pre-matrix-sign matrices are

\[ x_{00}=g_t,\qquad x_{10}=m_t^{\mathrm{K8}},\qquad x_{01}=C_t^{-1}g_t,\qquad x_{11}=C_t^{-1}m_t^{\mathrm{K8}}. \]

The metric is fixed at the evaluation state, so temporal filtering and curvature inversion commute in this analysis. Only matrices for which both inverse solves meet the same residual threshold are scored. Project each matrix into true-Hessian curvature ranks 1–8 and 9–24. If \(a_{ij,b}\) is the norm of matrix \(ij\) in band \(b\), mechanical factorization is measured by

\[ I_{\mathrm{gain},b} =\log a_{11,b}-\log a_{10,b}-\log a_{01,b}+\log a_{00,b}. \]

The certified bands factorize at all three measured checkpoints: the median \(I_{\mathrm{gain},b}\) remains within \(0.007\) of zero, and every interquartile range crosses zero. Thus the same-state damped inverse attenuates raw-gradient and K8 matrices by the same bandwise factor. This confirms the expected factorization in the certified frames; it does not establish whether the two operators perform a duplicated task.

Task-level redundancy requires a future-window loss. Let \(L_{ij,b}\) be the quadratic excess loss of the polar-normalized matrix relative to \(u_t^*\). Define

\[ I_{\mathrm{loss},b} =L_{11,b}-L_{10,b}-L_{01,b}+L_{00,b}. \]

Positive \(I_{\mathrm{loss},b}\) means substitution; negative values mean complementarity. At the late checkpoint, both certified bands are approximately zero and the full-space value leans complementary. This is descriptive only, because the associated kernel-ordering test fails.

The sparse objective cannot select kernels

The future window used checkpoints separated by 250 training steps. It assigns essentially the same score to lag zero, single EMA, bi-Maxwell, and K8 at all three measured states. At the early state, the four scores range only from 1011.2 to 1011.4. Momentum changes the intervening step-scale path, but the sparse objective observes only the distant local quadratics. It therefore aliases the kernel differences, and its \(I_{\mathrm{loss}}\) values cannot constrain kernel design.

The next falsifier uses densely serialized derivatives around each state; serialization is in progress as of 1 September 2026. Before scoring them, independent half-token damped solves at 131k and 524k tokens per half test whether curvature estimation is precise enough to distinguish kernels. If disagreement between halves remains larger than the kernel-score separation at the larger budget, the offline instrument is underpowered and stops.

Dense scoring begins with the known-ordering test. Reproducing K8 \(>\) bi-Maxwell \(>\) single EMA \(\approx\) no momentum would permit the bandwise \(I_{\mathrm{loss}}\) analysis. Another failure ends the hindsight-target framework rather than triggering a wider search.

Decision rule. Near-zero \(I_{\mathrm{gain}}\) and positive edge-band \(I_{\mathrm{loss}}\) would show that only the product of kernel edge response and metric edge attenuation is identifiable; one factor can then be fixed by normalization. Negative \(I_{\mathrm{loss}}\) retains separate temporal and curvature controls in that band.
