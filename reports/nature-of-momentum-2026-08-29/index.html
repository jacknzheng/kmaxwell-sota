Toward a principled theory of momentum

Research memo · 29 August 2026

Momentum is usually described as gradient smoothing. For Muon, that description misses its geometric role. Muon combines gradients across time and then applies an approximate matrix sign. The temporal combination can decide which matrix directions survive norm selection.

The NanoGPT experiments point toward a compact version of this mechanism. A scheduled single EMA recovers part of K-Maxwell's improvement. Six EMA states recover substantially more. Eight perform no better than six. The useful temporal response appears to move during training, yet its effective dimension is probably small.

Working hypothesis. Momentum in Muon selects among matrix-valued gradient patterns with different temporal structure. Scheduling helps because the competing patterns change over training. The intended destination is a momentum rule computed from measured optimizer state, with at most one meaningful setting exposed to the user.

1. The experimental clue

All methods in the main comparison use ordinary Muon through step 1000. The single-EMA method then moves its decay from 0.982 to 0.944. K6 and K8 move between mixtures of six and eight EMA states.

One time scale leaves a gapK6 improves substantially beyond the scheduled single EMA.
The gain saturatesK6 and K8 are tied at discovery resolution.

Direction matters: in a separate A100 fork, reversing the single-EMA schedule from 0.950 to 0.984 worsened validation loss by 0.0062 relative to bi-Maxwell. Long memory early and shorter memory late is the useful direction in the tested range.

The remaining K-Maxwell–EMA gap combines three effects: the current kernel, the momentum accumulated under earlier kernels, and the model trajectory created by those updates. The next experiment should isolate the current action of the kernel.

The power-law run adds little evidence. Its exponents were chosen to match K-Maxwell's mean lag, but the finite implementation produced mean lags of about 78 to 9 steps; the EMA comparator moved from about 52 to 16. It also amplified alternating gradients and white noise far more strongly. Its loss therefore says little about the power-law family. A properly matched power law remains untested, and rerunning it is lower priority than identifying what a kernel changes inside Muon.

2. Momentum as temporal selection before norm selection

Write a local gradient history as temporal Fourier components,

Gt=∑jRe⁡ ⁣(Ajeiωjt)G_t=\sum_j \operatorname{Re}\!\left(A_j e^{i\omega_j t}\right)Gt​=j∑​Re(Aj​eiωj​t)

where each matrix Aj carries a temporal frequency and a spatial orientation. A momentum kernel forms

The kernel response H(ω)=∑k≥0wke−iωkH(\omega)=\sum_{k\ge0}w_ke^{-i\omega k}H(ω)=∑k≥0​wk​e−iωk gives each temporal component its own amplitude change and phase shift. Muon then computes

Ut=msign⁡(Zt)U_t=\operatorname{msign}(Z_t)Ut​=msign(Zt​)

For the ideal matrix sign and any positive scalar c,

msign⁡(cZ)=msign⁡(Z),c>0\operatorname{msign}(cZ)=\operatorname{msign}(Z),\qquad c>0msign(cZ)=msign(Z),c>0

When one temporal component dominates, changing its amplitude has little effect on the Muon update. When two differently oriented components coexist, their relative amplitudes and phases determine the singular directions of their sum. The kernel changes their ratio through

H(ω1)H(ω2)\frac{H(\omega_1)}{H(\omega_2)}H(ω2​)H(ω1​)​

and can thereby rotate the update selected by Muon.

This mechanism makes scalar summaries such as mean lag and total noise gain incomplete descriptions. They integrate over temporal frequency and discard matrix orientation. A more relevant observable is a matrix-valued lag covariance: which gradient directions persist on each time scale, and how those directions compete in the matrix presented to Muon.

BF16 rounding and the numerical stabilizer break the exact scale invariance of the ideal matrix sign. Every proposed measurement below uses the deployed Newton–Schulz implementation directly.

3. Edge of stability supplies a boundary condition

The learning-rate multiplier ranges from 0.6 to 1.7, while its product with measured curvature ranges from 1.54 to 1.15. The precise constant drifts. The inverse trend is strong enough that a theory of momentum should explain how its filter interacts with the critical-curvature attractor.

The scalar calculation shows how a momentum kernel could move this boundary. On one quadratic direction, let gt = λxt and consider

xt+1=axt−ηλ∑k≥0wkxt−kx_{t+1}=a x_t-\eta\lambda\sum_{k\ge0}w_kx_{t-k}xt+1​=axt​−ηλk≥0∑​wk​xt−k​

where a is the multiplicative weight-decay factor. At the period-two boundary, the state flips sign each step: xt+1=−xt,xt−k=(−1)kxtx_{t+1}=-x_t,\quad x_{t-k}=(-1)^kx_txt+1​=−xt​,xt−k​=(−1)kxt​. Substitution gives

−1=a−ηλ∑k≥0(−1)kwk-1=a-\eta\lambda\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kw_k−1=a−ηλk≥0∑​(−1)kwk​

Define the response to an alternating signal as

F=∑k≥0(−1)kw(k)F=\sum_{k\ge 0}(-1)^k w(k)F=k≥0∑​(−1)kw(k)

The period-two boundary therefore satisfies

ηλcritF=1+a\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}F=1+aηλcrit​F=1+a

This is how momentum decay enters the period-two boundary in single-decay analyses. A multi-timescale kernel may reach the unit circle first at another frequency, so its full scalar boundary requires a scan over that circle. When period two binds, increasing F should reduce pinned curvature in inverse proportion if the scalar mechanism controls Muon.

The discrepancy is large enough to change the research priority. A richer fit of scalar critical-curvature formulas is unlikely to reveal the nature of Muon momentum. The matrix-selection hypothesis supplies a concrete missing mechanism: alternating response becomes a common amplitude when that component owns its singular directions, and Muon's normalization removes the amplitude. Its relative strength matters when other matrix patterns compete for those directions.

At early, middle, and late checkpoints, a common EMA bank represents the same raw gradient history. Candidate kernels are different linear combinations of that bank. Their response to a constant gradient, mean lag, alternating response, and white-noise gain are held within registered tolerances. The remaining degrees of freedom change temporal response while preserving the scalar explanations already under consideration.

Discovery batches select pairs that produce a large change in Muon's matrix-sign direction. Separate evaluation batches measure the rotation and its local optimization consequence. For Uw=msign⁡(Zw)U_w=\operatorname{msign}(Z_w)Uw​=msign(Zw​), second-order expansion gives

L(θ−ηUw)−L(θ)≈−η⟨g,Uw⟩+η22⟨Uw,HUw⟩L(\theta-\eta U_w)-L(\theta)\approx -\eta\langle g,U_w\rangle +\frac{\eta^2}{2}\langle U_w,\mathcal H U_w\rangleL(θ−ηUw​)−L(θ)≈−η⟨g,Uw​⟩+2η2​⟨Uw​,HUw​⟩

The first term measures descent alignment; the second measures curvature cost. Held-out batches at the shared checkpoint estimate both. This is a direct criterion for the kernel's action after Muon's norm selection.

The experiment begins with an offline response map across the three checkpoints. The pair with the clearest held-out difference then enters short continuations alongside duplicate controls. The continuation measures when the immediate direction change begins to affect sharpness and validation loss.

The first-step comparison is decisive because every input to the optimizer is shared until the coefficients are changed. The short continuations then measure the causal consequence of that intervention. Retrospective attribution of the original K-Maxwell gain would require a full factorial training study. This experiment answers the forward-looking question: does a local, measurable kernel-shape mechanism exist?

5. From an expressive basis to a small controller

K-Maxwell is useful as an identification basis. The final optimizer should use only the temporal states required by the identified mechanism.

If the same-state map finds kernels with better expected local progress, solve the local filter problem at checkpoints throughout training:

w∗(st)=arg⁡min⁡wE ⁣[−ηt⟨gt,Uw⟩+ηt22⟨Uw,HtUw⟩ | st]w^*(s_t)=\arg\min_w\mathbb E\!\left[ -\eta_t\langle g_t,U_w\rangle +\frac{\eta_t^2}{2}\langle U_w,\mathcal H_tU_w\rangle \,\middle|\,s_t\right]w∗(st​)=argwmin​E[−ηt​⟨gt​,Uw​⟩+2ηt2​​⟨Uw​,Ht​Uw​⟩​st​]

The state st contains measurements available to the optimizer: the learning rate, a curvature-margin estimate, and compact summaries of matrix-valued lag correlations. The filter is constrained to a small online EMA bank with bounded noise gain.

The expressive solutions form a dataset. We then ask which measured state predicts their variation and whether the solutions lie near a one-dimensional curve. A successful controller exposes that state target instead of training step. One plausible form mixes a fast and a slow EMA to hold a measured competition ratio at a fixed setpoint. The intervention data should select the state variable.

Edge of stability supplies a complementary test. A same-checkpoint, constant-learning-rate fork can vary alternating response F while keeping the other descriptors close. A curvature shift proportional to 1/F would make the kernel-corrected stability margin part of the controller. A small shift would leave curvature as environmental state while the kernel is chosen for matrix-mode selection.

  1. Map the same-state response. Test scalar-matched kernels at early, middle, and late checkpoints.
  2. Run short controlled continuations. Use the pair with the clearest held-out difference in Muon direction and predicted progress.
  3. Test the scalar stability correction. Run a separate same-state, constant-learning-rate fork that varies alternating response.
  4. Infer the state variable. Optimize expressive local kernels across checkpoints and regress their variation against measured state.
  5. Distill and validate. Implement the smallest EMA realization and test ordinary, high-learning-rate, and low-learning-rate trajectories.

Recommendation. Start with the same-state response map and short continuations. They decide whether the current theory contains a causal object worth compressing. More endpoint sweeps, another power-law fit, and finer K-Maxwell tuning can wait.

B. Curvature measurements and evidence limits

The steady points in the figure are (η, λ) = (0.60, 2.56), (0.77, 1.82), (1.15, 1.147), (1.30, 0.92), and (1.70, 0.675). Two lower-learning-rate runs failed the stated steady-regime criterion and were excluded.

Curvature is the median leading eigenvalue of each Muon matrix's diagonal Hessian block on a fixed batch, normalized to that matrix's step-1000 baseline. The full hybrid Muon–Adam update also depends on weight-norm adaptation, cross-block coupling, and finite Newton–Schulz effects.

C. Statistical scope

The training comparisons use one seed and one checkpoint fork per candidate. Confirmatory claims require repeated seeds under the Track 3 protocol. The momentum-free curvature comparison uses a separate trajectory whose weight norms are about 0.31 times the reference.
