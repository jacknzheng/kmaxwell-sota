Toward a principled theory of momentum

Research memo · 29 August 2026

We want a momentum rule whose temporal weighting follows the optimizer's measured state instead of a hand-designed training schedule. The central question is which property of recent gradient history should control that weighting.

Working hypothesis. In Muon, the useful state describes how gradient variation at different time scales is oriented within each weight matrix. Momentum changes the relative influence of those directions before the matrix-sign step.

1. A single EMA schedule captures one tenth of the K8 gain

Across four paired seeds, scheduling the decay of one exponential moving average (EMA) improves validation loss by about one tenth as much as K8, an eight-EMA K-Maxwell mixture. Both comparisons use bi-Maxwell, the matched two-EMA rule, as their control.

The comparison varies two freedoms in temporal weighting. Scheduling one EMA changes its memory length during training. K-Maxwell instead maintains several EMA summaries with different decay rates and schedules their mixture weights.

The remaining endpoint gap has three coupled sources: the temporal weights used at the end, the gradient summaries stored in the momentum buffers, and the model parameters produced by the preceding updates. Each can contribute to K8's lower validation loss. Endpoint training runs therefore establish that scheduling helps and leave the additional K-Maxwell gain causally unassigned.

2. Temporal weighting can become direction selection

The mechanism hypothesis is that different time scales occupy different matrix directions. Ideal matrix sign removes a common positive multiplier from its input, so temporal weighting matters when it changes the relative mixture of those directions.

3. Curvature constrains the theory

Changing the learning rate from several shared checkpoints produces the same aggregate response: after the fork, the median leading curvature of the Muon-managed matrices falls approximately as the inverse learning-rate multiplier. Independent forks near steps 1000, 1500, and 2400 give exponents near −1.2. The three-point step-2000 fork is directionally consistent but has not passed the same settled-window screen.

The median is unusually smooth because it averages over different matrix-level laws. Most Muon-managed matrices individually follow a tight power law in the learning-rate multiplier, but their slopes and vertical levels differ by much more than the estimated measurement noise. Their deviations lie on both sides of the median and largely cancel there.

The aggregate law hides a stable matrix-specific component. Curvature levels measured after different forks correlate strongly for the same matrix, even after conditioning on layer type. Fitted response exponents are much less stable.

Near-matched matrix gaps also reproduced across checkpoints, revealing a stable matrix-identity component beneath the slower drift. The measurement-step pattern suggests training-progress drift but does not establish it. Comparing early and late windows within the existing runs can test this interpretation without additional training.

Matrix type and block depth capture some structure but do not predict held-out absolute curvatures to the measurement floor. Removing the step-1000 normalization from the analysis leaves the same heterogeneity. Allowing each matrix its own exponent and level performs much better, yet still misses the estimated measurement floor and cannot predict an unseen matrix. Within each measured state, the current data therefore support matrix-specific power laws, with the variables controlling their slopes and levels still unidentified. Any predictive law must account for both the reproducible matrix-identity component and its drift over training.

The recorded variables do not yet predict the matrix-specific laws accurately. Matrix type, depth, shape, initial curvature, and raw-gradient norm leave large held-out errors and fail to explain the fitted constants to the measurement floor. Near-matched matrix pairs also exhibit clearly different fitted slopes or levels, so a smooth rule built from those fields is insufficient. The proxy tests also expose a gap in the instrumentation: the logs contain the raw gradient's norm and polar direction, while the candidate theories concern the scale and polar direction of the temporally filtered momentum matrix.

The next measurement should resolve a candidate state at the same per-matrix and training-time granularity: the singular values and directions of the momentum matrix before matrix sign, separated by the temporal components that produced it. This momentum spectrum is the time-scale-to-direction map proposed in Section 2. It could record both matrix identity and slow drift, but has not yet been shown to explain the curvature laws.

The fitted slope supports only a normalized trend comparison, not an absolute SGD threshold. An overlay of the rule ηλ = 2 requires physical learning-rate and curvature units. This equation makes two experimentally distinct predictions. First, hold the kernel fixed and vary the learning rate. The scalar boundary is λcrit = (2 − ηρ)/(ηF), which is approximately proportional to 1/η when ηρ is small. The runs above all use the same bi-Maxwell kernel, and hence the same F, but their post-fork trajectories are not held at the same state. They provide a descriptive slope comparison: the aggregate slope is close to −1, while the resolved deviation and differences among matrices require a richer empirical law.

Second, hold the learning rate and training state fixed and vary F: the scalar calculation predicts critical curvature proportional to 1/F. An early shared-state intervention now performs this test. Reducing F by factors of two and four produced no continuation-loss difference above duplicate-run variation. Its curvature measurements are still running, so the scalar stability prediction remains open.

Muon adds a directional complication: matrix sign removes common scale, but reweighting temporal components can rotate the matrix before that operation. The available momentum-free comparison cannot test the F prediction. It was measured late, after alternating energy had faded, and it changed the optimizer, stored history, and model trajectory together. Its details are in Appendix C.

4. Three scalar summaries do not determine Muon's update

We tested whether three numbers contain all the useful information in a temporal kernel: mean lag, the average age of the gradients it uses; alternating response F, the surviving amplitude of a gradient that flips sign every step; and white-noise gain, the output variance produced by independent unit-variance gradients. Two kernels matched on all three produce sharply different Muon updates and one-step losses from the same checkpoints. The remaining question is whether that local difference survives continued training.

Within a nonnegative mixture of the current gradient and EMA streams, fixing unit constant response, mean lag, F, and white-noise gain leaves essentially one kernel. Short moving averages create distinct kernels with the same summaries and require only a bounded ring buffer.

The displayed kernels differ only in how they distribute weight across gradient ages after normalization and the three summaries are fixed. The pair is re-solved for the finite gradient history available at each checkpoint; Appendix G gives the construction and numerical tolerances.

The experiment begins from a shared model, optimizer state, gradient history, and future minibatch order. It changes only the temporal weighting applied to the stored gradients. Duplicate continuations with the same kernel measure ordinary run divergence.

The effect may depend on training state, so the comparison repeats at checkpoints spanning training. At each checkpoint, matrix cosines and leading-subspace angles compare the updates produced by kernel A and kernel B from the identical state. One duplicate pair—two continuations using the same kernel, checkpoint, and data order—measures divergence from GPU arithmetic. Its difference defines the duplicate-control floor, which is reused at the other checkpoints; without such a floor, no observed difference is falsifiable. The duplicate fork-1500 arm in REQ-019 supplies an independent estimate at no additional cost.

The directional readout is the SVD of each momentum matrix Zt before matrix sign. For every stored temporal stream Ej, we record its coordinates in that singular basis, UTEjV; in particular, uiTEj vi measures how much time scale j feeds singular direction i. Both kernels are different linear combinations of the same recorded streams. Their coordinates therefore predict the update rotation under either reweighting, which can be compared with the realized rotation after matrix sign.

A fixed continuation resumes each fork for a number of steps registered before launch, then compares validation loss and curvature. A difference is resolved when it exceeds the corresponding duplicate-control floor. The update-angle floor begins near zero immediately after the fork and grows with the continuation horizon, so short windows give the sharpest test. If the floor grows large enough to absorb the kernel effect, that horizon is declared unusable rather than interpreted.

The local effect is large and persists across training states

The update rotation clears the duplicate-control floor at every checkpoint. Its one-step loss consequence is clear early and in the middle of training, then becomes small late in the cooldown. For this constructed pair, kernel A's flatter recent window gives a better gradient-alignment term than kernel B's long tail. One pair establishes a local direction in kernel space; it does not establish that flat windows are generally optimal.

The continuations show that this local effect is consequential and that one-step loss is not a safe selector. At the early and middle checkpoints, both matched kernels finish worse than the deployed bi-Maxwell kernel by more than duplicate-run divergence. Kernel A is the crucial counterexample: its first step improves loss relative to bi-Maxwell, but its continuation is worse. The late-checkpoint differences do not clearly exceed the floor.

The result selects the consequential branch of the registered test: mean lag, alternating response, and white-noise gain omit information that affects training over hundreds of steps. It does not select the finite-window implementation. Both matched kernels discard history older than 128 steps, while the deployed kernel retains a small slow tail. That shared truncation may explain why the deployed kernel beats both. It cannot explain the continuation gap between A and B, because they have the same support. The next experiment isolates recent-weight shape while holding the slow tail fixed.

The early realized-checkpoint comparison also calibrates the virtual evaluator in loss units. Parameter-level parity was calibrated after the original preregistered bound failed, so it is recorded as a post-hoc duplicate-floor calibration rather than a preregistered pass. The third-order diagnostic found no kernel-specific sharpening warning.

The one-step evaluator remains a precise local diagnostic. It measures how a temporal kernel changes the immediate update and decomposes the resulting loss change into gradient, curvature, and third-order terms. The continuation shows that none of these local terms is yet a validated selection objective.

A separate early-training fork tests the scalar stability theory. Between steps 1500 and 2000 the gradients still contain substantial period-two oscillation. Changing F while matching constant response, mean lag, and white-noise gain did not resolve a continuation-loss effect; the curvature readout will decide the narrower 1/F prediction.

Current decision. Construct a new matched pair whose two kernels share the deployed slow tail and differ only in their recent weights. This removes the common truncation in the present pair. Do not fit a state-conditioned controller until a local measurement predicts continuation outcomes.

The program advances only when each stage earns the next expense.

  1. Remove the tail-support confound. Match two kernels that share the deployed slow tail and differ in recent weighting, then repeat the short continuation. This decides whether the present pair failed because both kernels truncated history; estimated cost: 2–4 GPU-hours.
  2. Find a horizon predictor. Compare local measurements with continuation loss across a small, diverse kernel set. Continue only if a predictor ranks held-out kernels above duplicate uncertainty.
  3. Find the orientation statistic. Use the recorded momentum-SVD stream coordinates to find a compact matrix-valued readout that predicts update rotation and the successful horizon metric. Then test whether measured state predicts the selected kernel; offline analysis first.
  4. Separate identity from state. Predict persistent per-matrix curvature levels from static architecture and weight-spectrum features, and predict the residual drift from dynamic measurements. Cross-fork prediction is the test; begin offline.

Stop rule. Do not build a controller unless a cheap local measurement predicts continuation outcomes on held-out kernels and states. Defer more EMA-schedule tuning, aggregate curvature forks, and joint-norm theory while this test remains unresolved.

The end state is a minimal EMA bank governed by one measured quantity and exposing at most one user-facing hyperparameter.

B. Curvature measurements and evidence limits

The steady points in the figure are (learning-rate multiplier, normalized median curvature) = (0.60, 2.56), (0.77, 1.82), (1.15, 1.147), (1.30, 0.92), and (1.70, 0.675). Two lower-learning-rate runs failed the stated steady-regime criterion and were excluded. The accepted windows were steps 3000–3249, 2500–3000, 2125–2375, 1375–1625, and 1250–1500, respectively; the s = 1.30 window was still drifting downward at its edge.

Curvature is the median leading eigenvalue of each Muon matrix's diagonal Hessian block on a fixed batch, normalized to that matrix's step-1000 baseline. The transient window is 250 steps after a learning-rate raise or the step-1000 kernel switch, and 125 steps after a learning-rate drop. After that window, a segment is accepted when its whole-segment slope in log median curvature is within the length-scaled one-standard-deviation noise floor. All five points use 8-iteration Lanczos with the convergence correction and tail gate. Eight iterations underestimate the leading eigenvalue; the correction extrapolates the remaining gap by assuming geometric decay in successive Ritz-value increments. The tail gate drops matrix estimates whose extrapolated remaining error exceeds 5%. Each point is one segment from one trajectory; its uncertainty describes within-run measurement variation.

The geometric-tail extrapolation and tail gate were calibrated against 16- and 32-iteration references at early and late checkpoints. The corrected 8-iteration estimates had zero median bias at both ages; the change in bias between ages was less than 1% of the reported curvature drift. The one exception was embed.weight: its late 8-iteration estimate retained a −0.015 log10 under-correction, so its reported late-age values use 16 Lanczos iterations.

The law ladder fits absolute corrected eigenvalues. L1 uses one exponent and matrix-type levels; L2 adds depth-dependent levels; L3 allows type-specific exponents; L4 adds depth-dependent exponents; L5 gives every matrix its own level and exponent. Model selection uses held-out learning-rate runs, with a separate even-depth to odd-depth test. Fits exclude 81 of 360 Muon-matrix cells whose within-window drift exceeds three estimated standard errors; those cells are distributed across all depths. L5 is a diagnostic ceiling because it cannot predict an unseen matrix.

The recorded-feature check used matrix type, depth, shape, step-1000 curvature, and raw-gradient norm. The near-match test required the same matrix type, a depth difference of at most one block, and step-1000 curvature and gradient norm within 15%. Six such pairs differed by three to nine fitted standard errors in their law constants. This rejects a smooth, low-capacity rule over the tested neighborhoods. The proposed collapses based on raw-gradient scale and curvature along the raw-gradient polar direction were unsupported; the apparent scale relation largely vanished after controlling for the shared step-1000 state. The corresponding momentum-spectrum quantities were not recorded.

A second five-point bi-Maxwell set forked from step 2400. Its aggregate exponent was −1.19 ± 0.10, consistent with the earlier −1.21 to −1.28 estimates. For the two comparisons whose measurement windows differed by roughly 1,500 training steps, per-matrix log-curvature shifts had 0.5–0.7 root-mean-square magnitude, four to six times the pooled noise estimate; their residuals correlated at Pearson 0.73 and Spearman 0.86. The nearly step-matched comparison had a median offset of 0.03 and roughly twice the pooled noise. Fifteen of eighteen near-match gap comparisons agreed within 1.5 standard errors; all exceptions contained an unsettled cell. These observations motivate, but do not prove, the training-progress interpretation in the main text.

The learning-rate multiplier also scales decoupled weight decay in this harness. The full hybrid Muon–Adam update depends on weight-norm adaptation, cross-block coupling, and finite Newton–Schulz effects, so the plot is a descriptive inverse trend. BF16 rounding and the numerical stabilizer also break the exact scale invariance of ideal matrix sign. The intervention uses the deployed implementation.

A separate Frank–Wolfe calculation measured joint generalized sharpness over the product of the 72 Muon spectral-norm balls. Under the tested radius and loss-scaling convention, that quantity remained nearly constant from steps 1500 to 2750 while 2/η grew by a factor of 3.5. This rules out a direct transfer of that joint-norm formula under this convention; it does not rule out generalized-sharpness theories under a different norm or hybrid-optimizer treatment.
