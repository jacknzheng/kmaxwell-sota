Toward a principled theory of momentum

Research memo · 29 August 2026

In Muon, momentum acts through temporal averaging and matrix geometry. A temporal filter can rotate the matrix passed to the matrix-sign step and thereby change the update direction.

The NanoGPT runs show that changing one EMA helps, while six EMA states recover more of the gain. Six and eight states are tied at this discovery resolution. The runs leave three causes entangled: the current filter, the stored momentum history, and the model path created by earlier updates.

Working hypothesis. Momentum in Muon selects among matrix-valued gradient patterns with different temporal structure. Scheduling helps because the competing patterns change over training.

1. The experimental clue

Scheduling one EMA recovers about one third of the eight-state gain. The six-state K6 and eight-state K8 kernels are tied within the precision of these discovery runs. All candidates use the same Muon update through step 1000. The single-EMA decay then moves from 0.982 to 0.944. K6 and K8 combine EMA states with coefficients that change during training. K-Maxwell denotes this linear-combination family; K6 and K8 use six and eight states. The baseline is the matched bi-Maxwell control: the standard two-EMA kernel switched on at step 1000.

Against the same A100 bi-Maxwell control used for the power-law comparison, reversing the single-EMA schedule erased the gain and lost substantially. In this fork, longer memory early and shorter memory late outperformed the reverse schedule.

Section 4 changes only the current filter while holding the stored history and model state fixed.

The scheduled candidate targeted normalized taps wk ∝ (k + 1)−γ, with γ scheduled from 1.28 to 1.66, using a finite ten-EMA approximation. From the step-1000 switch to the end of training, the deployed finite-history kernel's average gradient age fell from about 78 to 9 steps; for comparison, the scheduled single EMA above fell from about 52 to 16. The nominal mean-lag match failed, and the candidate also gave much larger responses to alternating and noisy inputs. Test a corrected construction only after the same-state filter test.

2. Why a temporal filter can change Muon's direction

A filter changes Muon's direction when temporal components point in different matrix directions. Write the local gradient at step t as a sum of components, where Aj is the matrix amplitude and ωj is its temporal frequency:

Gt=∑jRe⁡ ⁣(Ajeiωjt)G_t=\sum_j \operatorname{Re}\!\left(A_j e^{i\omega_j t}\right)Gt​=j∑​Re(Aj​eiωj​t)

The matrix Aj sets the component's spatial orientation. For momentum weights wk, define the frequency response as H(ω)=∑k≥0wke−iωkH(\omega)=\sum_{k\ge0}w_ke^{-i\omega k}H(ω)=∑k≥0​wk​e−iωk. The matrix passed to Muon before matrix-sign normalization is

Zt=∑k≥0wkGt−k=∑jRe⁡ ⁣(H(ωj)Ajeiωjt)Z_t=\sum_{k\ge 0}w_kG_{t-k} =\sum_j\operatorname{Re}\!\left(H(\omega_j)A_je^{i\omega_jt}\right)Zt​=k≥0∑​wk​Gt−k​=j∑​Re(H(ωj​)Aj​eiωj​t)

Each component receives its own amplitude change and phase shift. Muon then computes

Ut=msign⁡(Zt)U_t=\operatorname{msign}(Z_t)Ut​=msign(Zt​)

For the ideal matrix sign and any positive scalar c,

msign⁡(cZ)=msign⁡(Z),c>0\operatorname{msign}(cZ)=\operatorname{msign}(Z),\qquad c>0msign(cZ)=msign(Z),c>0

A common positive rescaling of the entire filtered matrix disappears in the ideal matrix-sign normalization. Changing the ratio and phase between two differently oriented components can rotate the singular directions of their sum. For two components at frequencies ω1 and ω2, the filter changes their relative complex weight by

H(ω1)H(ω2)\frac{H(\omega_1)}{H(\omega_2)}H(ω2​)H(ω1​)​

Average lag and response to white-noise input are scalar summaries; neither records matrix orientation. The diagnostic therefore records each time scale's contribution to the matrix directions before the matrix-sign step.

3. Curvature suggests a candidate boundary condition

Across the accepted constant-learning-rate runs, measured equilibrium curvature rises as the learning rate falls. This resembles the edge-of-stability pattern and motivates a scalar reference calculation.

The product of learning-rate multiplier and measured curvature ranges from 1.54 to 1.15, so the inverse trend is clear while the constant drifts. A scalar test asks whether the filter contributes to this inverse relation. The plotted multiplier scales the base step size, so proportional changes in the multiplier are proportional changes in η below.

Setting Muon's matrix-sign step aside, a classical linear reference model predicts how the kernel changes critical curvature; Section 4 tests whether that prediction survives the matrix-sign normalization. On one quadratic direction, let gt = λxt and consider

xt+1=axt−ηλ∑k≥0wkxt−kx_{t+1}=a x_t-\eta\lambda\sum_{k\ge0}w_kx_{t-k}xt+1​=axt​−ηλk≥0∑​wk​xt−k​

The factor a is the multiplicative weight-decay factor. At the period-two boundary, the state flips sign each step: xt+1=−xt,xt−k=(−1)kxtx_{t+1}=-x_t,\quad x_{t-k}=(-1)^kx_txt+1​=−xt​,xt−k​=(−1)kxt​. Substitution gives

−1=a−ηλ∑k≥0(−1)kwk-1=a-\eta\lambda\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kw_k−1=a−ηλk≥0∑​(−1)kwk​

Define the response to an alternating signal as

F=H(π)=∑k≥0(−1)kwkF=H(\pi)=\sum_{k\ge 0}(-1)^k w_kF=H(π)=k≥0∑​(−1)kwk​

The period-two boundary therefore satisfies

ηλcritF=1+a\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}F=1+aηλcrit​F=1+a

For a single decay, this gives the period-two boundary. For a multi-timescale kernel, scan the other frequencies as well. If period two is the binding mode, the scalar model predicts λcrit∝F−1\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}\propto F^{-1}λcrit​∝F−1; the same-state comparison tests that prediction.

The scalar curvature prediction disagrees with the measured curvature in this separately trained model. The next test changes filter coefficients at a shared checkpoint and measures Muon's direction before the paths diverge.

4. A same-state test of matrix selection

K-Maxwell and the scheduled EMA arrive late in training with different filters, stored momentum, and model states. A coefficient-only intervention at a shared checkpoint isolates the filter's current action from stored history and model state.

At early, middle, and late checkpoints, two unchanged controls and two intervention branches copy the model weights, learning rate, weight decay, data order, Muon state, Adam state, and every other pre-fork state. A common EMA bank stores identical filtered summaries of the shared raw gradient history. Candidate kernels are different linear combinations of that bank. At each checkpoint, every candidate is normalized using the finite gradient history available there so H(0)=∑k≥0wk=1H(0)=\sum_{k\ge0}w_k=1H(0)=∑k≥0​wk​=1; a constant gradient passes unchanged and the common scale is fixed. Hold each candidate's average lag, response to sign-alternating inputs, and response to white-noise inputs within pre-specified tolerances. Report their drift over the fixed measurement window. The immediate branch difference then estimates the changed temporal response.

Discovery batches choose a pair predicted to rotate Muon's matrix-sign update. At the intervention step, Uw is computed from the shared training batch and history. Independent held-out batches estimate the gradient g, Hessian ℋ, and predicted one-step loss change for that fixed update. With Uw=msign⁡(Zw)U_w=\operatorname{msign}(Z_w)Uw​=msign(Zw​), second-order expansion gives

L(θ−ηUw)−L(θ)≈−η⟨g,Uw⟩+η22⟨Uw,HUw⟩L(\theta-\eta U_w)-L(\theta)\approx -\eta\langle g,U_w\rangle +\frac{\eta^2}{2}\langle U_w,\mathcal H U_w\rangleL(θ−ηUw​)−L(θ)≈−η⟨g,Uw​⟩+2η2​⟨Uw​,HUw​⟩

The first term measures descent alignment; the second measures curvature cost. Use this estimate to rank candidates; use the fixed continuation to test whether the ranking predicts loss.

Use the unchanged control's pre-normalization matrix as a common basis. For each EMA stream and intervention matrix, record the projection within that basis and the residual outside it. Measure update rotation with the cosine between flattened matrix-sign updates and the angle between their leading singular subspaces. These readouts identify which time scales changed the update direction.

The experiment begins with an offline response map across the three checkpoints. The pair with the clearest held-out difference then enters fixed-window continuations alongside duplicate controls. The continuation measures when the immediate direction change begins to affect sharpness and validation loss. A candidate pair advances only if the second-order predicted loss-change ordering has the same sign on every pre-specified evaluation batch. Call the continuation positive only if the branch difference exceeds the loss-and-curvature spread between the two unchanged controls over the same window.

5. From an expressive basis to a small controller

If the same-state test identifies a reproducible effect, retain the smallest EMA bank that preserves it. The implementation exposes at most one user-facing hyperparameter.

Let st contain measurements available to the optimizer: the learning rate, the measured curvature and its inverse-learning-rate reference, and the per-time-scale projection readouts from Section 4. Constrain the online EMA bank to a bounded response to white-noise input. Use the current gradient gt, Hessian ℋt, and candidate Muon update Uw to choose weights that minimize expected one-step loss change conditional on that state:

w∗(st)=arg⁡min⁡wE ⁣[−ηt⟨gt,Uw⟩+ηt22⟨Uw,HtUw⟩ | st]w^*(s_t)=\arg\min_w\mathbb E\!\left[ -\eta_t\langle g_t,U_w\rangle +\frac{\eta_t^2}{2}\langle U_w,\mathcal H_tU_w\rangle \,\middle|\,s_t\right]w∗(st​)=argwmin​E[−ηt​⟨gt​,Uw​⟩+2ηt2​​⟨Uw​,Ht​Uw​⟩​st​]

Use the optimized weight vectors at the three checkpoints as observations. Test whether one measured state variable predicts those weights, then whether the resulting rule predicts held-out rotation and loss or curvature. Keep only features that pass both tests at all three checkpoints.

At one shared checkpoint, vary alternating response F while holding the other summaries fixed. If curvature follows 1/F, include F in the stability model; otherwise prioritize matrix-direction features.

  1. Same-state response. Compare kernels matched on average lag and their responses to sign-alternating and white-noise inputs at early, middle, and late checkpoints.
  2. Post-fork outcome. Run the pre-specified fixed window and record first-step rotation, loss, and curvature.
  3. Scalar stability check. At one shared checkpoint, vary alternating response F while holding the other summaries fixed.
  4. State selection. Test whether one measured state variable predicts the optimized weights and held-out rotation or loss across all three checkpoints.
  5. Implementation check. Test the smallest EMA bank across seeds on baseline, high-learning-rate, and low-learning-rate trajectories.

Decision requested. Approve the same-state response map and its fixed continuation window. Defer another power-law fit, broad K-Maxwell tuning, and controller implementation until those measurements identify a reproducible state variable.

B. Curvature measurements and evidence limits

The steady points in the figure are (η, λ) = (0.60, 2.56), (0.77, 1.82), (1.15, 1.147), (1.30, 0.92), and (1.70, 0.675). Two lower-learning-rate runs failed the stated steady-regime criterion and were excluded.

Curvature is the median leading eigenvalue of each Muon matrix's diagonal Hessian block on a fixed batch, normalized to that matrix's step-1000 baseline. The transient window is 250 steps after a learning-rate raise or the step-1000 kernel switch, and 125 steps after a learning-rate drop. After that window, a segment is accepted when its whole-segment slope in log median curvature is within the length-scaled one-standard-deviation noise floor. All five points use 8-iteration Lanczos with the convergence correction and tail gate. Each point is one segment from one trajectory; its uncertainty describes within-run measurement variation.

The geometric-tail extrapolation and tail gate are a provisional analysis rule; calibration of its error model remains future work. The five points are descriptive results accepted under that rule.

The learning-rate multiplier also scales decoupled weight decay in this harness. The full hybrid Muon–Adam update depends on weight-norm adaptation, cross-block coupling, and finite Newton–Schulz effects, so the plot is a descriptive inverse trend. BF16 rounding and the numerical stabilizer also break the exact scale invariance of ideal matrix sign. The intervention uses the deployed implementation.

C. Statistical scope

The training comparisons use one seed and one checkpoint fork per candidate, so the one-third decomposition is a scale estimate. Published eight-seed results show gains from scheduled EMA mixtures on different stacks; they do not calibrate this one-seed decomposition. The same-state experiment uses duplicate controls at every checkpoint, and the distilled rule is evaluated across seeds. The momentum-free curvature comparison uses a separate trajectory whose weight norms are about 0.31 times the reference. The local Taylor diagnostic omits the explicit decay term and requires a smooth local approximation; the fixed continuation evaluates whether its ranking is useful.

D. Full scalar boundary for an arbitrary kernel

Substitute a trial mode xt = rt into the scalar update from Section 3. Dividing by xt gives

r−a+ηλ∑k≥0wkr−k=0r-a+\eta\lambda\sum_{k\ge0}w_kr^{-k}=0r−a+ηλk≥0∑​wk​r−k=0

The stability boundary is the first root to reach |r| = 1 as ηλ increases. Setting r = eiφ and scanning φ handles a power law, K-Maxwell, or any other realized finite kernel. The period-two formula in the main text is the φ = π case.
