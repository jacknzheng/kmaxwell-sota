Muon's edge remembers the gradient history

A long-memory momentum kernel lets curvature fall after the learning rate rises. A short-memory kernel with nearly the same period-two attenuation does not. Muon's edge of stability is therefore a property of the momentum–polar recurrence, not a scalar correction to Hessian sharpness.

Question

What quantity does momentum regulate when a normalized matrix-sign optimizer operates at the edge of stability?

The causal result

Kernel memory determines whether Muon compensates for a larger learning rate. Both comparisons start from the same saved model and optimizer state, use the same minibatches, and raise the learning rate by the same factor. The deployed kernel mixes gradient averages with average lags of roughly 6 and 49 steps. The short-memory comparison uses only the 6-step average.

The kernels attenuate a perfectly alternating gradient by almost the same amount. Their long-run responses are qualitatively different.

The short-memory run also loses the coherent period-two gradient field and ends with substantially worse validation loss. By 1,200 steps, its generalized curvature is 1.71 times the long-memory value. The effect is uniform across matrices. The long-memory component is carrying the feedback that flattens the bulk curvature seen by spectral descent. Matching the kernel's response at period two does not match that feedback.

Consequence

A theory based only on the kernel's period-two gain cannot explain the intervention. The state must include how the momentum buffers preserve the gradient field across time.

The recurrence supplies the stability object

Muon maintains several exponential averages of each gradient matrix. Their weighted sum is mixed with the current gradient, mapped to its polar factor, and used as a fixed-size update:

\[ m_{j,t}=\beta_jm_{j,t-1}+(1-\beta_j)g_t,\qquad p_t=(1-\alpha)g_t+\alpha\sum_j a_jm_{j,t}, \] \[ X_{t+1}=(1-\eta d)X_t-\eta s\,\operatorname{polar}(p_t). \]

Here \(m_{j,t}\) are the momentum buffers; \(\beta_j\) and \(a_j\) set their time constants and mixture weights; \(\alpha\) sets the total momentum mixture; \(d\) is weight decay; and \(s\) is Muon's rectangular-matrix scale.

A perturbation changes the weights, every momentum buffer, and the point at which the polar map is differentiated. Linearizing one matrix block gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta m_{j,t} &=\beta_j\delta m_{j,t-1}+(1-\beta_j)H_t[\delta X_t],\\ \delta p_t &=(1-\alpha)H_t[\delta X_t]+\alpha\sum_j a_j\delta m_{j,t},\\ \delta X_{t+1} &=(1-\eta d)\delta X_t-\eta s\, D\operatorname{polar}_{p_t}[\delta p_t]. \end{aligned} \]
Fast criticality. The local stability boundary is reached when the leading multiplier of this augmented recurrence has magnitude one. It depends on the Hessian, the momentum-buffer contents, the temporal kernel, and the realized pre-polar matrix \(p_t\). A scalar computed from the current Hessian omits three of those inputs.

This conclusion is also forced by scale invariance. Multiplying the loss by a positive constant multiplies every gradient and momentum buffer by that constant, while the exact polar direction stays unchanged. Any Hessian quantity that scales with the loss cannot be the complete critical invariant.

The alternating field changes curvature slowly

Fast oscillations still create a simple slow effect. Write the weights as a slowly moving center plus an alternating displacement:

\[ X_t=\bar X+(-1)^taZ. \]

A third-order expansion of the gradient contains an even term:

\[ g_t=\bar g+(-1)^taH[Z]+\frac{a^2}{2}D^3L[Z,Z,\cdot]+O(a^3). \]

When \(Z\) locally maximizes the Hessian quadratic form over the spectral-norm unit ball, Danskin's theorem identifies this third-order contraction with the gradient of the corresponding generalized curvature,

\[ M(\bar X)=\max_{\lVert Y\rVert_2\le1}\langle Y,H(\bar X)[Y]\rangle, \qquad \nabla M=D^3L[Z,Z,\cdot]. \]

Let \(q\) be the alternating component of the matrix presented to the polar map. Averaging the two polar updates sends the even term through the derivative of that map:

\[ \Delta_{\rm cubic}\bar X \simeq -\eta s\frac{a^2}{2} D\operatorname{polar}_{q}[\nabla M]. \]

The polar factor is the gradient of the nuclear norm. Its derivative is therefore the Hessian of a convex function and is positive semidefinite wherever the polar factor is differentiable. This term reduces \(M\) along the directions visible to that derivative. Ordinary training may sharpen or flatten \(M\); the observed level is where that drift balances the oscillation-induced flattening.

The derivation holds for a frozen active direction. Rotation of that direction contributes another term whose sign is not fixed. The calculation supplies a flattening mechanism, not a proof that one scalar stays constant.

The alternating field is directly measurable. Raising the learning rate increases median cycle work from 221 to 390, and an independent replay reproduces 391 despite rapid trajectory divergence. The ratio of cycle work to slow work barely changes, however, and its registered cross-matrix prediction of later curvature velocity has the wrong sign. Coherent work is therefore part of the mechanism, but that normalized ratio is not its state variable.

The constant 2 has a narrow meaning

For the actual shape-scaled update \(U_t=s\,\operatorname{polar}(p_t)\), the quadratic one-step loss change is

\[ \Delta L_{\rm quad} =-\eta\langle g_t,U_t\rangle +\frac{\eta^2}{2}\langle U_t,H_t[U_t]\rangle. \]

Define the state-conditioned ratio

\[ \mu_{{\rm loss},t} =\frac{\langle U_t,H_t[U_t]\rangle} {\langle g_t,U_t\rangle}. \]

The quadratic loss change is zero when \(\eta\mu_{\rm loss}=2\). This equality defines a local loss boundary. It does not show that training selects the boundary.

A single-mode oscillation makes the ambiguity precise. If its displacement uses a fraction \(\kappa\) of the maximum allowed by the normalized step, then

\[ \eta\mu_{\rm loss}\simeq\frac{2}{\kappa}. \]

Curvature cancels from this approximation. A ratio near 2 can therefore mean that the oscillation nearly saturates the step budget. The replay analysis must compare the ratio with an independent measure of \(\kappa\) before treating it as evidence about sharpness.

Measurement in progress

One-step replays record the actual momentum-conditioned update at saved optimizer states. Fixed-token Hessian products will measure \(\eta\mu_{\rm loss}\), the independently estimated oscillation saturation, and the corresponding ratio for the instantaneous-gradient polar direction.

Generalized curvature is a bulk quantity

The spectral-norm geometry combines many Hessian directions. The first completed checkpoints place the generalized spectral-ball curvature between roughly eleven and fourteen times the top Euclidean Hessian eigenvalue for the median matrix. Individual matrix ratios range from about two to fifty-seven.

Muon uses normalized updates, so the raw value \(M(H)\) has no universal \(2/\eta\) prediction. Without momentum, the corresponding upper diagnostic is \(sM(H)/\lVert g\rVert_*\). Momentum replaces the nuclear-gradient denominator with the alignment between the raw gradient and the history-conditioned polar direction.

What the next measurements decide

The current evidence supports a memory-dependent curvature feedback and rejects a threshold correction based only on period-two gain. The remaining measurements decide whether this mechanism admits a useful low-dimensional state.

Current answer

Momentum is part of the optimizer's stability state. Its useful role here is to preserve a coherent gradient field long enough for third-order geometry to regulate curvature, while the polar map limits how that field enters each update. A principled momentum rule should therefore respond to measured temporal coherence and curvature feedback rather than follow a fixed schedule or a single Hessian threshold.
